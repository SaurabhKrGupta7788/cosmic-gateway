How It Works

Unveiling the mechanics behind our exoplanet classification model.

The Mission: Classifying Exoplanets

Our universe is vast, with billions of stars, many of which host their own planets. These planets outside our solar system are called exoplanets. The primary goal of this tool is to use a sophisticated machine learning model to analyze data from potential exoplanets and classify them as either a "Confirmed" planet or a "Candidate" that requires further study.

The model is trained on data from the Kepler Space Telescope. It analyzes the light from a star, looking for tiny, periodic dips in brightness. These dips, or "transits," can indicate that a planet is passing in front of its star. The data you provide—like KOI Period (how long the planet takes to orbit its star) and KOI Depth (how much the star's light dims)—are the key features our model uses to make its prediction.

Hyperparameter Tuning: You are the Scientist

Take control of the model's architecture to see how its predictions change.

A machine learning model isn't just a black box; it's a complex system defined by its structure and learning strategy. The settings that control this are called hyperparameters. By adjusting them, you can fundamentally change how the model learns and performs. Our tuning page allows you to experiment with these core components.

Number of Hidden Layers

This determines the model's "depth." A simple model might have 1 or 2 layers. A deeper model (e.g., 5 layers) can learn much more complex and abstract patterns from the data. However, too many layers can sometimes lead to "overfitting," where the model becomes too specialized in the data it has already seen.

Neurons per Layer

This is the model's "width." Each neuron acts as a small computational unit. More neurons in a layer allow the model to learn more features at that specific stage. Increasing neurons can boost performance, but adding too many can make the model unnecessarily complex and slow to train.

Dropout Percentage

This is a powerful technique to prevent overfitting. During training, a certain percentage of neurons are randomly "ignored" or "dropped out." This forces the other neurons to learn more robustly, as they can't rely on any single neuron. A dropout of 20% means 1 in 5 neurons are randomly deactivated at each training step.

Learning Rate

This is arguably one of the most important hyperparameters. It controls how much the model adjusts its internal weights after each prediction during training. A high learning rate can help the model learn faster, but it might "overshoot" the best solution. A very low rate is more precise but can make training extremely slow.
